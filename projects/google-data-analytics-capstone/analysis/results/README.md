# Analysis Results

This folder contains output files generated by the capstone analysis scripts.

These files represent derived analytical results, not raw source data.

## Current Results

### `ntia_age_technology_use.csv`

Weighted percentage estimates of technology use by age group using the NTIA 2023 Internet Use Survey working dataset.

The analysis compares:

- Laptop use
- Desktop computer use
- Tablet use
- Smartphone use
- Internet use at home

Age groups:

- 3–17
- 18–34
- 35–49
- 50–64
- 65–74
- 75+

#### Key Finding

Among adults, reported technology use generally declines with age.

The largest differences are visible in:

- Laptop use
- Smartphone use
- Internet use at home

The 3–17 group is treated separately because technology-use behavior among children differs from adult use patterns.

### `ntia_income_technology_use.csv`

- $75,000–$99,999: 81.6%
- $100,000–$149,999: 83.1%
- $150,000+: 83.8%

These results indicate a strong association between household economic resources and reported technology use.

### `ntia_age_income_technology_use.csv`

Weighted percentage estimates of technology use by both age group and household/family income.

Age groups:

- 18–64
- 65+

Income groups:

- Under $25,000
- $25,000–$49,999
- $50,000–$74,999
- $75,000–$99,999
- $100,000–$149,999
- $150,000+

#### Key Finding

Within every income group, adults age 65 and older report lower laptop, smartphone, and home-internet use than adults age 18–64.

The lowest observed technology-use levels occur among adults age 65+ in households earning under $25,000:

- Laptop: 24.3%
- Smartphone: 58.3%
- Internet at home: 55.9%

Technology use also generally rises with household income within both age groups.

These results suggest that age and household income are both associated with technology use and that older adults with lower household incomes may experience particularly low levels of reported technology use.

This analysis is descriptive and does not establish a statistical interaction effect or causal relationship.


### `ntia_age_digital_engagement.csv`

Weighted percentage estimates of selected online activities by age group using the NTIA random-respondent sample.

Age groups:

- 18–64
- 65+

Activities:

- Email
- Online financial services
- Online government services
- Online education or job training

#### Key Finding

Adults age 65 and older report lower participation in all four measured online activities than adults age 18–64.

The largest differences are:

- Education / job training: 28.5% vs 8.5%
- Financial services: 81.6% vs 63.2%
- Email: 94.3% vs 86.5%
- Government services: 42.2% vs 34.9%

These results indicate lower digital engagement among older adults across several online activities, particularly financial services and education or job training.

This analysis measures reported online activity, not digital skill or literacy.

The random-respondent survey weight `PWPRMWGT` is used for these estimates.

### `ntia_texas_vs_us.csv`

Weighted comparison of selected adult technology-use and digital-engagement indicators for Texas and the United States.

Indicators:

- Laptop use
- Smartphone use
- Internet use at home
- Online financial services
- Online government services

#### Key Finding

Texas estimates are broadly similar to national estimates across the selected indicators.

Observed Texas differences from the U.S. estimate are:

- Laptop use: -0.2 percentage points
- Smartphone use: +2.8 percentage points
- Internet use at home: +1.7 percentage points
- Financial services: +1.9 percentage points
- Government services: -0.4 percentage points

These results do not support describing Texas as having an unusually large statewide digital-use gap based on these measures.

Instead, the stronger disparities identified in this analysis appear within demographic and economic subgroups, particularly by age, income, and educational attainment.

The technology-use estimates use `PWSSWGT`.

The digital-engagement estimates use `PWPRMWGT`.



### `openrepair_category_outcomes.csv`

Repair outcomes for 47,024 technology-related repair records from the Open Repair Alliance dataset.

#### Key Finding

A majority of repair attempts in every selected technology category resulted in either a completed fix or an assessment that the item remained repairable.

The highest combined fixed-or-repairable shares were:

- Mobile: 81.5%
- Laptop: 81.3%
- Desktop computer: 74.8%
- Tablet: 71.8%
- Games console: 70.8%
- PC accessory: 70.3%
- Battery / charger / adapter: 69.4%
- TV and gaming-related accessories: 64.0%
- Printer / scanner: 61.5%
- Flat screen: 58.7%

`Repairable` does not mean that a repair was completed. It indicates that reasonable next steps toward repair were identified.

The dataset represents devices brought to participating repair events and should not be treated as representative of all discarded electronics or the general U.S. population.



### `openrepair_end_of_life_barriers.csv`

Documented repair barriers for technology-related Open Repair Alliance records that reached an end-of-life outcome.

Across the selected technology categories:

- End-of-life records: 11,560
- Records with a documented repair barrier: 3,883
- Barrier documentation rate: 33.6%

#### Key Finding

Among end-of-life technology records with a documented barrier:

- Repair information not available: 22.9%
- Spare parts not available: 18.9%
- Spare parts too expensive: 18.1%
- Item too worn out: 15.7%
- No way to open product: 12.6%
- Lack of equipment: 11.8%

Most documented barriers therefore relate to repair information, parts, cost, equipment, or product accessibility rather than the item simply being too worn out.

Because only 33.6% of end-of-life records contain a documented barrier, these percentages should be interpreted only among records where a barrier was reported.

The dataset represents devices brought to participating repair events and is not representative of all discarded electronics.

### `openrepair_outcomes_by_year.csv`

Annual repair outcomes for selected technology categories in the Open Repair Alliance dataset from 2016 through 2024.

Years before 2016 were excluded because of relatively small record counts. 2025 was excluded because the source dataset contains only a partial year through July.

#### Key Finding

The share of technology repair records classified as fixed or repairable declined from 78.1% in 2016 to 71.3% in 2024.

Over the same period, the share classified as end of life increased from 14.7% to 26.5%.

The pattern is not strictly linear:

- 2016: 78.1% fixed or repairable; 14.7% end of life
- 2017: 73.6%; 16.8%
- 2018: 70.2%; 21.9%
- 2019: 70.4%; 22.7%
- 2020: 70.9%; 25.0%
- 2021: 72.1%; 25.9%
- 2022: 73.5%; 24.6%
- 2023: 72.2%; 25.2%
- 2024: 71.3%; 26.5%

These changes should not be interpreted as proof that electronic devices became less repairable over time. The composition of participating repair organizations, countries, product categories, and devices may also have changed during the period.


### `warm_reuse_scenario.csv`

Illustrative greenhouse-gas scenario calculated using U.S. Environmental Protection Agency Waste Reduction Model (WARM) Version 16 factors for portable electronic devices.

Scenario assumptions:

- Devices reused: 500
- Assumed average device weight: 2.5 kg
- Total device mass: 1,250 kg
- Total device mass: 1.3779 short tons

Modeled results:

- Reuse / source reduction instead of landfill: 41.13 MTCO2e avoided
- Recycling instead of landfill: 1.49 MTCO2e avoided

The device count and average device weight are illustrative assumptions, not measured program data.

The emissions factors come from EPA WARM Version 16. Results are modeled scenarios rather than observed environmental outcomes.


## Method

Percentages are calculated using the NTIA person-level survey weight `PWSSWGT`.

Only records eligible for each technology-use question are included in the denominator.

Survey responses are coded as:

- `1` = Yes
- `2` = No
- `-1` = Not in universe

Records coded `-1` are excluded from percentage calculations rather than treated as "No."

## Interpretation Notes

These analyses identify associations in survey data.

They do not demonstrate that age or income directly causes differences in technology use.

The NTIA variables used here measure reported technology use and should not automatically be interpreted as direct measures of device ownership or household access.
