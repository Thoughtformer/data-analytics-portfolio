# Recommendation Framework

## Decision Question

Should a community organization, municipality, library system, foundation, or nonprofit invest in an integrated community technology initiative combining:

- donated-device collection
- device repair and refurbishment
- youth technical training
- redistribution of usable technology
- personalized digital support
- digital-literacy education
- fraud-awareness assistance
- responsible electronics recycling

---

## Recommendation

The available evidence supports proceeding with a **limited pilot program** rather than either rejecting the concept or immediately attempting full-scale implementation.

The evidence is sufficient to establish that the major components of the model are individually plausible and have been combined successfully in comparable programs.

The evidence is not sufficient to determine the program's local:

- demand
- donation volume
- repair yield
- operating cost
- staffing requirements
- participant cost
- financial sustainability
- employment outcomes

A pilot is therefore the appropriate next decision stage because it can test the variables that cannot be reliably estimated from existing public evidence.

---

## Why a Pilot Is Supported

### 1. A measurable access and engagement need exists

Analysis of the 2023 National Telecommunications and Information Administration Internet Use Survey found substantial differences in technology use and digital engagement across income, age, and education groups.

Examples include:

- household laptop use ranging from 41.2% among households below $25,000 in income to 84.6% among households at $150,000 or more
- substantially lower laptop, smartphone, and home-internet use among lower-income adults age 65 and older compared with adults ages 18–64
- lower laptop use among youth in lower-income households

These findings support targeting services toward populations experiencing measurable technology-access or engagement disparities.

---

### 2. Many devices brought into repair settings retain useful repair potential

The Open Repair Alliance analysis included 23,612 laptops, desktop computers, mobile phones, and tablets.

Outcomes were:

- Fixed: 52.9%
- Repairable: 26.3%
- End of life: 17.1%
- Unknown: 3.7%

A combined 79.2% were either fixed or assessed as repairable.

These results do not estimate the repairability of all discarded electronics.

They do establish that substantial repair potential exists among devices entering community repair environments.

---

### 3. Some repair barriers may be addressable operationally

Among core access devices that reached end of life and had a documented barrier:

- spare parts too expensive: 24.4%
- repair information unavailable: 19.8%
- spare parts unavailable: 16.1%
- lack of equipment: 14.7%
- no way to open product: 13.1%
- item too worn out: 11.9%

Only 11.9% of documented barriers were attributed simply to the item being too worn out.

This suggests that some unsuccessful repairs may involve constraints potentially affected by:

- tool access
- technician training
- repair documentation
- parts sourcing
- donor-device harvesting
- standardized repair procedures

The data does not establish how often removing these barriers would result in a successful repair.

---

### 4. Refurbishment can function as a training environment

The Computers for Schools Plus and Computers for Schools Intern programs demonstrate that device refurbishment can operate alongside structured youth technical training.

During the evaluated period:

- more than 475,000 refurbished devices were distributed
- 1,674 youth interns participated
- 96% of surveyed interns reported hardware repair and refurbishment skills
- 86% reported problem-solving skills

OurCycle LA provides a U.S.-based precedent in which refurbishment was similarly connected with workforce development.

This supports testing refurbishment not only as a device-recovery activity but also as a hands-on training environment.

---

### 5. Device access is stronger when paired with human support

The Tech Allies randomized pilot combined:

- devices
- internet access
- repeated one-on-one instruction
- volunteer assistance

Participants receiving the intervention significantly increased their technology use.

The evidence therefore supports testing a service model that combines hardware access with personalized assistance rather than relying on device distribution alone.

---

### 6. Fraud prevention is relevant but should be designed carefully

Older adults can experience substantial losses from fraud and are disproportionately represented in some scam categories, including tech-support scams.

The intervention evidence is mixed.

Brief scam-identification instruction does not reliably make participants accurate at identifying fraudulent messages.

However, targeted and repeated interventions have produced improvements in:

- caution
- confidence
- fraud awareness
- observed revictimization in one randomized trial

A pilot should therefore test fraud support as a combination of:

- education
- safe-response habits
- trusted human assistance

rather than promising that participants will become capable of independently identifying every scam.

---

### 7. Comparable programs demonstrate operational feasibility

Two particularly relevant precedents are:

#### Computers for Schools Plus

Integrated:

- donated technology
- refurbishment
- youth technical training
- redistribution
- reuse

#### OurCycle LA

Integrated:

- surplus municipal computers
- refurbishment
- workforce training
- device distribution
- broadband access
- digital-literacy education
- electronic-waste reduction

These programs demonstrate that the proposed components can operate within coordinated service models.

They also document challenges involving:

- funding
- staffing
- outreach
- partner coordination

A pilot should therefore test not only service outcomes but also the operating model itself.

---

## Recommended Pilot Scope

The initial pilot should remain deliberately limited.

A practical pilot could contain four connected service tracks.

### Track 1 — Device Recovery and Refurbishment

Activities:

- accept donated or surplus computers and related technology
- inspect and inventory incoming devices
- securely erase storage devices
- evaluate repairability
- refurbish viable devices
- harvest usable parts from nonviable donor devices when appropriate
- responsibly recycle unrecoverable equipment

Primary measures:

- devices received
- devices evaluated
- devices successfully refurbished
- devices used for parts
- devices recycled
- repair yield
- average repair time
- average refurbishment cost

---

### Track 2 — Youth Technical Training

Activities:

- supervised hardware inspection
- device cleaning
- component replacement
- operating-system installation
- basic troubleshooting
- data-erasure procedures
- inventory documentation
- customer-support fundamentals

Primary measures:

- trainees enrolled
- trainees completing the program
- training hours
- devices worked on per trainee
- demonstrated technical competencies
- certifications or additional education pursued
- employment or education status at follow-up

The pilot should measure employment outcomes but should not assume that training causes employment.

---

### Track 3 — Device Access and Digital Support

Activities:

- distribute eligible refurbished devices
- assist with initial setup
- provide basic digital-literacy instruction
- help participants access common online services
- provide personalized troubleshooting
- refer participants to available broadband-support programs when appropriate

Primary measures:

- devices distributed
- participants served
- support sessions delivered
- repeat-support visits
- participant-reported confidence
- participant-reported ability to complete selected digital tasks
- service satisfaction

---

### Track 4 — Trusted Fraud and Scam Support

Activities:

- teach common scam patterns
- teach safe verification practices
- explain remote-access risks
- provide a trusted place to ask whether a suspicious message or alert appears legitimate
- provide reporting and referral information

Primary measures:

- participants receiving fraud-awareness support
- suspicious communications reviewed
- participant confidence in verification procedures
- knowledge of safe-response behaviors
- referrals to appropriate fraud-reporting resources

The pilot should not claim to prevent fraud unless victimization outcomes are directly measured.

---

## Pilot Population

The evidence supports prioritizing groups that show measurable access or engagement disparities.

Potential pilot populations include:

- lower-income households
- older adults
- youth from lower-income households
- individuals with limited access to full computers
- residents seeking foundational digital skills

Eligibility criteria should ultimately be determined by:

- available resources
- partner organizations
- local demand
- program capacity

The pilot should avoid unnecessarily restrictive eligibility rules until local demand is better understood.

---

## Pilot Scale

The first pilot should be large enough to generate useful operational data but small enough to remain manageable.

The initial objective should not be maximum participation.

The objective should be to determine:

> Can the model operate effectively and sustainably under local conditions?

A pilot should therefore prioritize measurement quality over scale.

---

## Core Pilot Metrics

The pilot should establish a consistent data-collection system before services begin.

### Device Pipeline

Track:

- devices donated
- device type
- device condition
- repair attempted
- repair completed
- repairable but not completed
- end of life
- repair barrier
- parts cost
- labor time
- final disposition

### Financial Metrics

Track:

- direct program expenses
- equipment expenses
- parts expenses
- recycling expenses
- staffing costs
- donated goods or services
- cost per device refurbished
- cost per device distributed
- cost per participant served

### Training Metrics

Track:

- enrollment
- completion
- instructional hours
- competencies achieved
- participant retention
- employment status
- education status
- follow-up outcomes

### Digital-Support Metrics

Track:

- participant age group
- household-income category when voluntarily reported
- device access
- internet access
- support sessions
- type of assistance requested
- repeat visits
- pre/post confidence or task-completion measures

### Environmental Metrics

Track:

- number of devices reused
- weight of devices reused
- number of devices recycled
- weight of devices recycled

These measurements would allow actual pilot data to replace the assumptions currently used in the EPA WARM scenario analysis.

---

## Environmental Scenario Benchmark

The capstone's illustrative WARM analysis modeled:

- 500 portable electronic devices
- 2.5 kg average assumed weight
- 1,250 kg total device mass
- 1.3779 short tons

Using EPA WARM Version 16 factors, the scenario produced:

- 41.13 MTCO2e avoided for reuse/source reduction instead of landfill
- 1.49 MTCO2e avoided for recycling instead of landfill

These are not predicted pilot outcomes.

They demonstrate how actual device-count and weight data collected during the pilot could be converted into a reproducible environmental-impact estimate.

---

## Cost Benchmark

The Digital Literacy Exchange Program provides useful comparison points but demonstrates substantial cost variation.

Across 36 projects:

- project costs ranged from $3 to $3,165 per participant
- the arithmetic mean was $394
- the median project fell within the $201–$300 range
- the overall program-level estimate was $73 per participant after expanded reach

This distribution shows that delivery model and scale strongly influence participant cost.

The proposed pilot should therefore calculate its own:

- cost per participant
- cost per refurbished device
- cost per distributed device
- cost by service track

rather than adopting a single benchmark from another program.

---

## Pilot Decision Criteria

At the end of the pilot, decision-makers should evaluate whether the program merits:

- expansion
- redesign
- continuation at pilot scale
- discontinuation

The decision should be based on observed local evidence.

Important questions include:

### Device Supply

- Is there a reliable stream of suitable donated technology?
- What percentage can be economically refurbished?

### Demand

- Do residents use the services?
- Which services receive the greatest demand?

### Cost

- What does each service actually cost?
- Are costs stable enough to forecast?

### Training

- Can trainees safely and effectively perform useful technical work?
- Do participants complete the program?
- Are skills demonstrably gained?

### Access

- Are refurbished devices reaching residents with genuine technology-access needs?
- Do participants use the devices and support services?

### Operations

- Can staffing, supervision, inventory, security, and partner coordination be managed reliably?

### Sustainability

- Can grants, donations, partnerships, earned revenue, or other funding realistically support continued operation?

---

## Decision Logic

The evidence suggests the following decision sequence:

```text
PUBLIC EVIDENCE SHOWS NEED
            ↓
PUBLIC EVIDENCE SHOWS MODEL FEASIBILITY
            ↓
COMPARABLE PROGRAMS SHOW OPERATIONAL PRECEDENT
            ↓
IMPORTANT LOCAL VARIABLES REMAIN UNKNOWN
            ↓
RUN A LIMITED, MEASURED PILOT
            ↓
COLLECT LOCAL COST, DEMAND, REPAIR, TRAINING,
ACCESS, AND ENVIRONMENTAL DATA
            ↓
EVALUATE WHETHER EXPANSION IS JUSTIFIED
