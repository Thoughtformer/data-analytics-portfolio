# Comparable Program: OurCycle LA

## Program

OurCycle LA was a City of Los Angeles digital-inclusion initiative launched in 2015.

The program used surplus City computers that otherwise would have entered the City's electronic-waste stream.

Its model combined:

- collection of surplus computers
- computer refurbishment
- refurbishment and recycling job training
- employment opportunities for people facing barriers to employment
- distribution of refurbished computers to low-income residents
- affordable or free internet access
- digital-literacy training
- responsible recycling of equipment that could not be reused

## Why This Program Is Relevant

OurCycle LA is particularly relevant to this capstone because several components of the proposed community technology initiative were implemented together within one U.S. municipal program.

The City described its goals as simultaneously:

- maximizing use of existing computers
- reducing electronic waste
- providing job training
- creating employment opportunities
- improving access to technology
- helping bridge the digital divide

This provides a domestic precedent for treating device reuse, workforce development, digital access, and digital literacy as parts of the same operating model.

---

## Pilot Structure

OurCycle LA was organized as a public-private partnership involving:

- the City of Los Angeles
- nonprofit organizations
- social enterprises
- schools
- community organizations
- corporate partners

Surplus City computers were evaluated for reuse.

Usable equipment was refurbished and distributed, while equipment that could not be recovered was directed toward recycling.

Qualified residents receiving devices were also connected with digital-literacy training and low-cost internet options.

---

## Pilot Outcomes

The April 2016 OurCycle LA final report documented the following outcomes:

| Outcome | Actual |
|---|---:|
| Computers refurbished | 2,600 |
| Computers demanufactured and properly recycled | 3,231 |
| Youth/adults trained in refurbishment-related work | 97 |
| Jobs created | 10 |
| Participants completing digital-literacy training | 863 |
| End users receiving refurbished computers | 777 |
| Computers distributed to nonprofit organizations | 413 |
| New high-speed internet subscribers at home | 579 |

The program exceeded its original goals for:

- computers refurbished
- people trained in refurbishment work
- jobs created

Several digital-inclusion targets were not reached during the initial pilot.

---

## Later Program Activity

A 2018 City of Los Angeles update reported that the expanded program had:

- refurbished and distributed more than 3,000 PCs to low-income families and nonprofit organizations
- trained more than 100 youth in computer refurbishment and hardware/software configuration
- created 10 temporary and full-time jobs
- provided digital-literacy training to nearly 1,000 families or individuals
- provided more than 500 refurbished computers and hotspot devices to low-income households with K–12 students
- distributed nearly 200 refurbished computers to seniors in senior housing and community centers

This later activity demonstrates that the model continued beyond the initial pilot period.

---

## Integrated Operating Model

OurCycle LA provides a particularly useful precedent because the same stream of surplus technology supported several community objectives.

The model can be summarized as:

surplus municipal computers
→ device evaluation
→ refurbishment training and employment
→ refurbished devices
→ distribution to underserved residents
→ internet access and digital-literacy support
→ recycling of unusable equipment

Rather than operating separate technology-access, workforce-development, and environmental programs, OurCycle LA connected them operationally.

---

## Workforce Development

The pilot trained 97 youth and adults in refurbishment-related work.

The final report stated that this represented 129% of the program's original training goal.

Ten jobs were created during the pilot period.

The City later reported that more than 100 youth had received training involving:

- computer refurbishment
- hardware configuration
- software configuration

These outcomes demonstrate that refurbishment work can function as a hands-on technical training environment.

They do not establish long-term employment outcomes for trainees.

---

## Digital Access and Literacy

The program did more than distribute computers.

Its digital-inclusion model also incorporated:

- digital-literacy instruction
- low-cost or free internet options
- broadband adoption
- partnerships with housing and community organizations

The 2016 pilot reported:

- 863 digital-literacy completions
- 777 end users receiving refurbished computers
- 579 new home high-speed internet subscribers

Later City reporting indicated that distribution expanded to families with school-age children and older adults.

---

## Environmental Component

The program also treated refurbishment as an electronic-waste strategy.

During the initial pilot:

- 2,600 computers were refurbished
- 3,231 computers were demanufactured and properly recycled

The City reported that the number of devices sent directly to recycling was lower than originally expected because more of the surplus computers were considered potentially refurbishable.

This is relevant to the capstone's preference for:

reuse when practical
→ recycling when reuse is not practical

rather than treating all surplus electronics immediately as waste.

---

## Important Implementation Lessons

The final report also documents difficulties that are useful for designing a future program.

### Funding

The pilot reported insufficient funding for several parts of the project.

Some partners provided substantial pro-bono support, but the report found that relying heavily on unpaid partner resources created operational problems.

The report recommended securing sufficient funding for all program components before implementation.

### Staffing

The City concluded that the pilot would have benefited from a full-time project manager dedicated specifically to the program.

Existing staff were often managing OurCycle LA in addition to other responsibilities.

### Outreach

Participation and outreach proved more difficult than expected.

The project did not meet several original goals involving:

- digital-literacy participation
- device distribution
- nonprofit distribution
- new broadband subscriptions

The program eventually expanded its outreach strategy through schools, community organizations, and the Housing Authority of the City of Los Angeles.

### Program Planning

The final report recommended more extensive planning with experienced industry partners before launching a similar program.

These challenges are particularly valuable for the capstone because they show that technical feasibility alone does not guarantee successful community participation or program delivery.

---

## Capstone Relevance

OurCycle LA provides a U.S.-based precedent for nearly the entire integrated model considered in this capstone.

It demonstrates that one program can combine:

- surplus-device recovery
- refurbishment
- youth and adult technical training
- job creation
- device distribution
- broadband access
- digital-literacy education
- service to low-income residents
- service to older adults
- electronic-waste reduction

This complements the other evidence in the capstone:

- NTIA data identifies disparities in technology use and digital engagement.
- Open Repair data demonstrates substantial repair potential among devices brought to community repair events.
- Computers for Schools Plus demonstrates refurbishment combined with youth workforce development at larger scale in Canada.
- Tech Allies provides evidence for personalized technology training among older adults.
- OurCycle LA demonstrates that many of these components have already been integrated within a U.S. municipal program.

OurCycle LA therefore provides evidence of operational feasibility.

It does not establish that an identical program would achieve the same results in another city or that its observed outcomes were caused solely by the program.

---

## Limitations

OurCycle LA was a specific Los Angeles program operating within a large municipal government and partnership network.

Its experience should not be treated as directly representative of a smaller Texas community.

The program's own final report documented several unmet goals and implementation difficulties.

The reported outcomes demonstrate program activity and feasibility rather than causal impact.

The principal outcome reports are also historical, primarily covering activity from 2015 through 2018.

This source is therefore best used as a comparable-program precedent rather than evidence of current Los Angeles conditions.
