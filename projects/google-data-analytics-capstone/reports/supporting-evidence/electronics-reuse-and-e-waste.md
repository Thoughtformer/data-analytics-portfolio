# Electronics Reuse and E-Waste Evidence

## Purpose

This evidence note examines the environmental rationale for including electronic-device repair, refurbishment, and reuse in the proposed community technology initiative.

---

## Global E-Waste Generation

The Global E-waste Monitor 2024 reported that the world generated approximately 62 million tonnes of electronic waste in 2022.

This represented approximately 7.8 kilograms of e-waste per person globally.

Only 22.3% of the total e-waste mass was documented as formally collected and recycled in an environmentally sound manner.

The report projects that global e-waste generation could reach approximately 82 million tonnes by 2030 under current trends.

The report identifies several factors contributing to continued e-waste growth, including:

- increasing consumption of electronic devices
- technological change
- shorter product life cycles
- limited repair options
- product-design limitations
- inadequate e-waste management infrastructure

---

## Materials and Resource Loss

Electronic waste contains substantial quantities of reusable materials.

The Global E-waste Monitor estimated that e-waste generated in 2022 contained approximately:

- 31 million tonnes of metals
- 17 million tonnes of plastics
- 14 million tonnes of other materials such as minerals, glass, and composites

The report estimated the value of metals contained in 2022 e-waste at approximately $91 billion globally.

Formal e-waste management also reduces the need for additional primary-material extraction.

The report estimated that documented recycling activity in 2022 avoided approximately 900 million tonnes of primary ore extraction.

---

## Reuse Before Recycling

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency identifies reuse and refurbishment as important components of sustainable electronics management.

According to the EPA, reusing and refurbishing electronics:

- extends product life
- keeps usable equipment out of the waste stream longer
- reduces demand for raw materials
- creates opportunities to reduce environmental impacts across the product life cycle

The EPA describes waste prevention and reuse as preferable to recycling when reuse remains practical.

Recycling remains important when continued use, repair, or refurbishment is no longer viable.

---

## Why Repair and Refurbishment Matter

Repair and refurbishment can delay the point at which a device becomes waste.

This creates a different environmental pathway than immediate disposal:

usable or repairable device
→ repair or refurbishment
→ extended useful life
→ delayed replacement and disposal
→ eventual responsible recycling

This does not mean every repair automatically produces a net environmental benefit.

The environmental result depends on factors such as:

- remaining useful life
- repair materials and replacement parts
- transportation
- energy efficiency of the older device
- whether reuse actually displaces purchase of another device
- end-of-life handling

However, reuse and refurbishment are recognized components of sustainable electronics management because they can extend product life and reduce demand for newly manufactured materials.

---

## Relationship to Open Repair Findings

The environmental case becomes more relevant when combined with the capstone's Open Repair analysis.

Among 23,612 repair-event records involving laptops, desktops, mobile phones, and tablets:

- 52.9% were fixed
- 26.3% were assessed as repairable
- 79.2% were either fixed or repairable

These Open Repair results do not establish the environmental impact of each repair.

They do show that a substantial share of devices entering participating community repair events retained repair potential.

Combined with EPA guidance on product-life extension, this supports treating refurbishment and reuse as a plausible waste-reduction component of the proposed program.

---

## Capstone Relevance

The environmental component of the proposed community technology initiative is not based simply on recycling more electronics.

The stronger model is:

donated or surplus device
→ evaluate for reuse
→ repair or refurbish when practical
→ redistribute for continued use
→ responsibly recycle equipment that cannot be recovered

This approach can connect several goals:

- extending the useful life of electronic devices
- reducing avoidable electronic waste
- reducing demand for new raw materials
- supplying devices for digital-access programs
- providing practical repair experience for youth trainees

The environmental benefit therefore complements the program's digital-access and workforce-development functions rather than operating as an unrelated activity.

---

## Limitations

Global e-waste statistics describe worldwide waste generation and should not be interpreted as estimates of local electronic waste in Texas or the proposed service area.

The Global E-waste Monitor primarily measures e-waste generation, collection, and recycling rather than the outcome of individual community repair programs.

The EPA provides evidence and guidance supporting reuse and refurbishment but does not imply that every repaired device produces the same environmental benefit.

This capstone therefore treats electronic reuse as a supported environmental strategy while avoiding claims about specific emissions or waste reductions that have not been directly modeled.
