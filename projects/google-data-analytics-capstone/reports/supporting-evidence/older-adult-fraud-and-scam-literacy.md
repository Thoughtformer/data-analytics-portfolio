# Older-Adult Fraud Risk and Scam-Literacy Evidence

## Purpose

This evidence note examines whether fraud and scam prevention is a reasonable component of a community technology-support program, particularly for older adults.

It also evaluates whether scam-awareness education has been shown to improve people's ability to identify fraudulent digital communications.

---

## Fraud Losses by Age

Federal Trade Commission Consumer Sentinel data for 2024 show an important distinction between the frequency and severity of reported fraud losses.

Older adults were generally less likely than younger adults to report losing money to fraud.

However, when older adults did report a monetary loss, the median loss increased substantially with age.

Median reported fraud losses in 2024 were:

- Ages 60–69: $691
- Ages 70–79: $1,000
- Ages 80 and older: $1,650

For comparison, the median reported loss for adults ages 40–49 was $500.

This suggests that older adults should not simply be described as more likely to fall for fraud overall. The available FTC data instead show that older adults can experience substantially larger financial losses when fraud does result in monetary harm.

Only 45% of fraud reports in the 2024 Consumer Sentinel dataset contained usable age information, so these figures should not be interpreted as population-level fraud prevalence estimates.

---

## Tech-Support Scams

FTC analysis also shows that some fraud types disproportionately affect older adults.

Using 2024 loss reports normalized by population, adults age 60 and older were approximately five times as likely as younger adults to report losing money to a tech-support scam.

Older adults were also more likely to report losses from several other fraud categories, including:

- prize, sweepstakes, and lottery scams
- romance scams
- government impersonation scams

Other scam types, including job scams and some investment scams, were reported at higher rates among younger adults.

### Interpretation

The evidence supports targeting scam education toward specific risks faced by older adults rather than assuming that older adults are uniformly more susceptible to every type of fraud.

Tech-support scams are especially relevant to a community technology-support program because they directly exploit uncertainty about computers, devices, security warnings, and technical assistance.

---

## Scam-Education Experiment

A 2026 study published in *Experimental Aging Research* examined whether short scam-prevention interventions improved the ability of younger and older adults to distinguish legitimate emails from scam emails.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of three conditions:

- no intervention
- read-only scam education
- interactive scam education

Before and after the intervention, participants evaluated both legitimate and fraudulent emails.

### Findings

The study found no significant improvement in detection sensitivity following either educational intervention.

In other words, participants did not become significantly better at accurately distinguishing legitimate emails from scam emails.

However, participants in both educational conditions became more cautious and showed a greater tendency to classify emails as suspicious.

The researchers concluded that the intervention may encourage caution even when it does not measurably improve scam-detection accuracy.

---

## Interpretation

This study provides an important limitation for the proposed program.

Fraud-prevention education should not be presented as a proven method for teaching people to reliably identify scams.

A more defensible program goal would be to help participants develop safer behaviors, such as:

- slowing down before responding to unexpected messages
- independently verifying requests
- avoiding unsolicited remote-access requests
- recognizing common tech-support scam patterns
- knowing where to seek trusted technical assistance
- reporting suspicious communications
- avoiding irreversible payment methods when a request appears suspicious

Education may encourage greater caution even when participants cannot consistently distinguish every fraudulent message from a legitimate one.

---

## Capstone Relevance

Fraud prevention fits naturally within the older-adult technical-support component of the proposed community technology initiative.

The evidence supports two separate conclusions:

1. Older adults can face substantial financial consequences from fraud and are disproportionately represented in some scam categories, particularly tech-support scams.
2. Scam-awareness education alone should not be assumed to produce accurate scam detection.

A community program could therefore focus on both education and access to trusted human assistance.

Rather than expecting participants to independently identify every scam, the program could provide a trusted place to ask:

> "Is this message, warning, phone call, or computer alert legitimate?"

This approach is consistent with the broader capstone model of combining technology access with personalized support rather than relying on information or hardware alone.

---

## Limitations

FTC Consumer Sentinel statistics are based on reports submitted to the FTC and its data contributors.

They do not represent every fraud incident, and many frauds are never reported.

Age information was available for only 45% of 2024 fraud reports.

The 2026 scam-intervention study examined email judgments in an experimental setting. Its findings should not automatically be generalized to every scam type or real-world financial decision.

The intervention also increased caution without significantly improving scam-detection sensitivity, so it should not be cited as evidence that brief scam education makes participants reliably accurate at detecting fraud.
